Methodology

How the Buying Score is calculated

The score isn't a rating average and it isn't a black box. Here is exactly what goes into it.

Every product page you analyse produces a single number from 0 to 100. That number is built from three independently assessed components, each scored on its own before being combined. We publish the weighting because a score you can't interrogate isn't worth acting on.

40%

Value

Current price measured against the product's own 12-month history and against comparable listings for the same item. A permanent "50% off" that never moves scores as full price, because that is what it is.

35%

Quality

What reviewers actually report about build, materials, accuracy of the listing photos and whether the item survives past the first few weeks — drawn from review text, not star counts.

25%

Reliability

Seller track record, return and warranty terms, shipping claims versus reported reality, and how much of the review base looks organic.

Why star ratings are the weakest input

A 4.6-star average tells you almost nothing on its own. It compresses a thousand different experiences into one number, it is trivially inflated by incentivised reviews, and it is heavily weighted toward the earliest buyers — who are typically the most enthusiastic and who bought a version of the product that may since have changed.

So the star average is treated as one weak signal among many. The heavier signals come from the review text: which specific complaints recur, whether they cluster around a particular date (often a sign the manufacturer changed a component), whether positive reviews describe the product in concrete detail or in generic language, and how the ratio of detailed-to-generic reviews compares to the norm for that category.

How review-trust checking works

Fake and incentivised reviews leave patterns. The review-trust check looks for the ones that survive translation across marketplaces:

  • Timing clusters. Organic reviews arrive on a long tail. A burst of dozens of five-star reviews inside a few days, especially shortly after listing, is not how real purchase behaviour looks.
  • Language uniformity. Genuine reviewers write about their own use case in their own words. Paid reviews converge on the same phrases, the same length, and the same structure.
  • Rating shape. A healthy product shows a J-curve — mostly high ratings with a real tail of low ones. A product with almost no middle and no low ratings has usually had its low ratings suppressed or its high ratings manufactured.
  • Listing reuse. On some marketplaces a seller can swap the product behind an established listing while keeping its review history. Reviews describing a completely different item than the one on sale are a strong signal that this has happened.
  • Verified-purchase ratio. Weighted differently per platform, because the meaning of "verified" differs a great deal between Amazon and, say, AliExpress.

The output is an authenticity percentage plus the specific reasons behind it. You always see why, not just the number.

What each score band means

ScoreVerdictWhat it means in practice
80–100 Buy Fairly priced against its own history, quality reports are consistent, and the review base looks genuine. No meaningful reason to wait.
60–79 Buy with conditions Sound product, but something is off — usually the price is above its normal band, or a specific recurring complaint applies to some buyers. The report names the condition.
40–59 Wait Typically a price that has been higher than usual for weeks, or a product whose recent reviews are noticeably worse than its older ones. Waiting is usually the better move.
0–39 Avoid Serious problems: heavily manipulated reviews, a seller with a poor track record, a fake discount, or recurring reports of the item failing quickly.

Why the analysis differs per store

A single scoring model applied identically to every marketplace produces bad results, because the marketplaces are not comparable. On Amazon, verified-purchase status and seller reputation are meaningful and available, so they carry real weight. On AliExpress the same fields exist but mean much less, while shipping time and item-as-described disputes matter far more — so those move to the front. On Shein, sizing is the single largest source of returns, so the model prioritises whether reviewers report the item running small or large and whether the photos match what arrived. On Alibaba the buyer is usually a business, so per-unit price at minimum order quantity, bulk tiers and supplier verification displace review volume almost entirely.

What the score is not. It is an assessment of a listing at a point in time, based on publicly visible information on that page. It is not a guarantee, not financial advice, and not a substitute for your own judgement about whether you need the item at all. Prices and listings change; a score from last month may not hold today.

Where the price history comes from

Price history is assembled from observed listing prices over time for the specific product identifier, up to 12 months where available. Coverage is better on large catalogues that have been tracked longer, and thinner on newly listed items — where there is not enough history to be useful, the report says so rather than drawing a confident-looking line through two data points.

The practical use of this is spotting anchored discounts. A "was $199, now $99" claim is only a discount if the item has actually sold at $199 recently. A large share of the permanent-sale listings you see have not.

Limits worth knowing

  • The extension reads the visible text of the product page you are on. If a page hides key detail behind interactions the extension can't see, that detail is not in the analysis.
  • Brand-new listings with a handful of reviews cannot be assessed for review authenticity with any confidence. The report will tell you the sample is too small rather than invent a percentage.
  • Very niche or specialist categories — industrial parts, medical devices, some regulated goods — have thin comparable data, and the score should be treated as a rough prior at best.
  • AI analysis can be wrong. Treat the report as a well-informed second opinion, not a verdict.

Frequently asked questions

Everything people ask before installing. If yours isn't here, email us — a person answers.

Do I need an account or an API key?

No. Install the extension, open a product page, click Analyse. There is no sign-up, no email required and no API key to paste. You only give us an email address if you buy Pro, and that comes from Stripe at checkout.

What do I get on the free plan?

Five full AI analyses every day — the complete report, not a teaser — plus unlimited basic analyses, the coupon finder, the watchlist and AI chat. The daily count resets at midnight in your local time.

It's free permanently, not a trial that expires.

What does Pro actually add?

Unlimited full analyses, and the three components that make up the score in detail: the Buying Score breakdown, the review-trust check with its reasoning, and 12-month price history with buy-or-wait advice.

If you shop occasionally, the free plan is genuinely enough. Pro is for people analysing several items a day.

Does it slow down my browsing?

No. Nothing runs until you open the extension on a product page and ask for an analysis. It does not run in the background, does not inject anything into pages you haven't asked about, and adds nothing to page load.

What data do you collect?

When you request an analysis, the visible text of that product page is sent to our server, analysed, and discarded — it isn't stored or tied to any identity. We don't collect your browsing history, location or IP, and we don't sell data.

Full detail is in the privacy policy.

How accurate is the AI?

It's a well-informed second opinion, not an oracle. It's strongest where there's plenty of public signal — popular electronics, homewares, fashion — and weakest on brand-new listings or niche categories with little history. When confidence is low, the report says so instead of guessing.

Do affiliate commissions affect my score?

No. Buy links may be affiliate links and we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you. The scoring model has no access to which merchants pay us — it can't favour them because it doesn't know.

We'd rather tell you to skip a purchase and keep you using the tool.

Which browsers are supported?

Chrome, and Chromium-based browsers that install from the Chrome Web Store — Edge, Brave, Opera, Arc and Vivaldi. There is no Firefox, Safari, iOS or Android version yet.

Which currency are the prices in?

Pro is billed in Australian dollars — A$7 per month or A$49 per year. Your card issuer converts at their rate if you're paying from another country, so the amount on your statement will differ slightly.

Any time, from the billing portal link in your licence email, or by emailing us. Cancelling stops the next renewal and you keep Pro until the end of the period you've paid for. No cancellation fee, no retention phone call.

Yes — within 7 days of purchase, for any reason, no questions asked. Email contact@daba-enterprise.com and we'll process it. Details in the refund policy.
